What is the ex-dividend date?

The ex-dividend date is an important date that helps determine whether a shareholder will receive an upcoming dividend payment.

In general, if you buy a stock on or after its ex-dividend date, you will not receive the next scheduled dividend. The seller will receive it instead.

If you're new to dividend investing, you can also explore the dividend investing glossary for more beginner-friendly terms.

How does the ex-dividend date work?

Companies that pay dividends announce several important dates. The ex-dividend date is the point when a stock begins trading without the right to receive the upcoming dividend.

For example, suppose a company's ex-dividend date is June 15. An investor who buys the shares on June 14 may be eligible for the upcoming dividend, assuming the trade settles normally.

An investor who buys the shares on June 15 or later would generally not receive that dividend payment.

What are the main dividend dates?

Dividend announcements commonly include several dates that serve different purposes.

  • Declaration date: The date the company announces the dividend.
  • Ex-dividend date: The date the stock begins trading without the right to the upcoming dividend.
  • Record date: The date the company checks its shareholder records to determine who is entitled to the dividend.
  • Payment date: The date the dividend is actually distributed to eligible shareholders.

These dates are related, but they are not interchangeable. Understanding the difference can help prevent confusion about when a dividend will be received.

Why does the stock price sometimes change?

A stock may open lower on its ex-dividend date because new buyers are no longer entitled to the upcoming dividend.

In theory, the share price may adjust by roughly the amount of the dividend, although actual market prices are also affected by normal trading activity, company news, investor sentiment, and broader market conditions.

This is one reason a dividend should not be viewed as free money. Receiving a dividend does not automatically increase an investor's total wealth by the amount of the payment.

Should you buy a stock just before the ex-dividend date?

Buying a stock only to capture an upcoming dividend does not guarantee a profit.

Share prices can move before and after the ex-dividend date, and taxes, transaction costs, market conditions, and other factors may affect the outcome.

For long-term investors, the quality of the company, dividend sustainability, financial strength, and total return may be more useful considerations than focusing on a single dividend payment.

How does this relate to dividend yield?

The ex-dividend date tells you about eligibility for a specific dividend payment. Dividend yield tells you how a company's annual dividend compares with its current share price.

These are different concepts, but understanding both can give you a clearer picture of how dividend investing works.

You can learn more in What Is Dividend Yield? and What Is Dividend Payout Ratio? .

What about dividend ETFs?

Dividend-paying ETFs also have ex-dividend dates, record dates, and payment dates.

The same general idea applies: an investor typically needs to own the ETF before its ex-dividend date to be eligible for the upcoming distribution.

Key takeaway

The ex-dividend date helps determine who receives an upcoming dividend. If you buy shares on or after that date, you generally will not receive the next dividend payment.

Knowing the ex-dividend date, record date, and payment date can make dividend schedules easier to understand, but these dates should be considered alongside the broader fundamentals of an investment.
