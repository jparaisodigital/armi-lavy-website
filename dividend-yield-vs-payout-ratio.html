Dividend yield vs. payout ratio: what's the difference?

Dividend yield and payout ratio are both commonly used when reviewing dividend-paying companies, but they answer two different questions.

Dividend yield focuses on the dividend relative to the stock price. Payout ratio focuses on how much of a company's earnings are being distributed as dividends.

The difference in one sentence

Dividend yield helps describe the dividend income relative to the price of the investment, while payout ratio helps show how much of the company's earnings are being used to fund that dividend.

Because they measure different things, one should not be used as a substitute for the other.

What does dividend yield tell you?

Dividend yield compares a company's annual dividend per share with its current share price.

Dividend Yield = Annual Dividend Per Share ÷ Share Price × 100

For example, if a stock trades at $100 and pays $4 per share in annual dividends, its dividend yield is 4%.

Yield can be useful when comparing the dividend income offered by different investments.

But a high yield is not automatically a sign of a better investment. Yield can rise simply because a stock's price has fallen.

For a deeper explanation, read What Is Dividend Yield?

What does payout ratio tell you?

Payout ratio compares the dividend with the company's earnings.

Dividend Payout Ratio = Dividends Per Share ÷ Earnings Per Share × 100

If a company earns $5 per share and pays $2 per share in dividends, its payout ratio is 40%.

That means 40% of those earnings are being distributed as dividends, while the remainder is retained by the company.

For the full explanation, read What Is a Dividend Payout Ratio?

A simple side-by-side example

Imagine a company with the following numbers:

  • Share price: $100
  • Annual dividend per share: $4
  • Earnings per share: $8

Its dividend yield would be 4% because the company pays $4 in annual dividends for every $100 of current share price.

Its payout ratio would be 50% because the $4 dividend represents half of the company's $8 in earnings per share.

The same dividend therefore creates two different percentages because each metric compares it with something different.

Why can a high dividend yield be misleading?

Dividend yield moves when either the dividend or the share price changes.

If a stock price falls sharply while the dividend remains unchanged, the calculated yield rises.

That can make the investment look more attractive based on yield alone even when the falling price reflects concerns about the company's business or dividend.

This is one reason investors often look beyond yield before drawing a conclusion.

Why can a high payout ratio matter?

A higher payout ratio means more of the company's earnings are being distributed to shareholders rather than retained.

That does not automatically make the dividend unsafe. What is normal can differ across companies and industries.

However, a company paying out most or all of its earnings may have less room to absorb weaker results without changing the dividend.

A payout ratio above 100% means the dividend is greater than current earnings under that calculation, which deserves additional review.

Should you look at yield or payout ratio first?

There is no universal order that works for every investor or every company.

Yield may help answer: "How large is the dividend relative to today's share price?"

Payout ratio may help answer: "How much of the company's earnings are being used for the dividend?"

Looking at both can provide more context than looking at either one alone.

Neither metric tells the whole story

Dividend yield and payout ratio are useful starting points, but they do not provide a complete picture of a company's financial health.

Investors may also review factors such as:

  • Earnings trends
  • Free cash flow
  • Debt levels
  • Dividend history
  • Business conditions
  • Industry characteristics

Dividends can also be reduced or discontinued, so neither metric guarantees future dividend payments.

Key takeaway

Dividend yield and payout ratio measure different parts of the dividend picture. Yield compares the dividend with the stock price, while payout ratio compares the dividend with company earnings.

Reviewing both can provide more context, but neither metric should be treated as a complete measure of investment quality or dividend safety.
